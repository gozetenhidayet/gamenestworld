How the AI Thinks

The computer opponent evaluates its replies by looking two moves ahead (a minimax search with alpha-beta pruning) and by weighing piece values and board position -- pawns are worth 1 point, knights and bishops 3, rooks 5, and queens 9, with a small positional bonus for well-placed pawns.

Rules Coverage

Standard piece movement is fully supported, and a pawn reaching the last rank automatically promotes to a queen. Castling and en passant capture aren't implemented in this version, so plan your king's safety and pawn play without relying on either.

Strategy

Because the AI only looks two moves ahead and doesn't need to account for castling, tactics that pay off three or more moves later slip past it more easily than they would against a stronger engine -- look for concrete two-move tactics like forks, pins, and back-rank threats rather than long positional plans.

Common Mistakes

Leaving a piece defended by only one unit is risky -- the AI's evaluation reliably spots and takes an undefended piece. Assuming you can castle to safety is the other common miscalculation, since it isn't available in this version.

History

Chess's rules trace back over a thousand years to a game called chaturanga in India, evolving through Persia and medieval Europe into essentially its modern form by around 1475 -- it remains one of the most studied and computer-analyzed games ever created.
